The short version

Rent − vacancy − repairs, capital reserves, management − property tax (Michigan investor rate), insurance, HOA, utilities, fees = Net operating income (NOI) − mortgage payment = Cash flow

A listing makes our list only if it earns at least per door per month and at least cash-on-cash per year with our default assumptions.

1

Rent

For each unit we use, in order:

  1. The current lease, when the listing reports one.
  2. HUD’s Small Area Fair Market Rent for the unit’s ZIP code and bedroom count (—).
  3. HUD’s county-wide Fair Market Rent when the ZIP isn’t covered.

HUD Fair Market Rents are a conservative, public, verifiable benchmark (roughly the 40th percentile of local rents). They’re not a rent opinion — a renovated unit may rent for more, a tired one for less. You can type your own rent for any unit. HUD’s Fair Market Rent data.

2

Vacancy & reserves

We set aside a share of the gross rent every month, even when nothing is broken:

  • Vacancy: — empty months between tenants and unpaid rent.
  • Repairs: — the everyday leaky faucet and broken window.
  • Capital reserves: — saving for the big replacements: roof, furnace, water heater.

These are lean defaults suited to a solid, hands-on owner. Older West Michigan housing often deserves more — you can raise any of them on a property’s page, and we flag pre-1940 and pre-1978 homes.

3

Management

Our default assumes you manage the property yourself (0%). Flip the “Hire a property manager” switch on any property to add a professional manager at of collected rent, plus a leasing fee of about of a month’s rent each time a new tenant signs (spread over a typical -year tenancy).

Out-of-state? Plan on professional management — and judge the deal with it turned on.

4

Michigan property tax — the big surprise

The tax on a listing is what the seller pays, and in Michigan it’s usually far lower than what an investor will pay. Two reasons:

  • Uncapping. While someone owns a home, its taxable value can only rise slowly. When it sells, taxable value resets (“uncaps”) to about of the sale price.
  • No homestead exemption. Owner-occupants get the Principal Residence Exemption from roughly 18 school operating mills. Rentals don’t, so they pay the higher non-homestead rate.
Investor tax per year ≈ price × × rental millage ÷ 1,000

Millage comes from the — county millage tables for each city or township we cover, with a county-wide fallback when a city isn’t in our table. Rates differ by school district, and a mailing address may sit in a different township — always confirm the parcel.

5

Insurance, utilities & fees

  • Insurance (landlord policy): about + of the price + per extra unit, per year. Condos and townhomes pay a fraction since the association insures the building. Get a real quote.
  • Owner-paid utilities: for single-family homes and condos (tenant pays); per unit per month for 2–4 unit buildings, where the owner often pays shared water and sewer.
  • Rental registration & inspections: per unit per month.
  • HOA dues come straight from the listing.

Cash needed = down payment + closing costs (+ any rehab budget). Cash-on-cash = one year of cash flow ÷ cash needed.

7

The two tests

A property passes only when it clears both:

  • per door per month — a cushion for the surprises every landlord gets.
  • cash-on-cash — a fair return on the money you actually put in.

Why both? A big down payment can make $/door look great while your cash earns little; a small down payment can show a strong percentage on a deal with no margin for error.

    Limitations — please read

    • All listings currently shown are hypothetical sample data. They demonstrate the calculator and are not properties for sale.
    • Every figure is an estimate. Actual rents, taxes, insurance, repairs and loan terms will differ.
    • We don’t inspect properties. Condition, deferred maintenance, rental-certification requirements and lead-paint rules can change the numbers dramatically.
    • Cash flow ignores appreciation, loan paydown and income-tax effects (depreciation, etc.).
    • Condo and townhome associations may limit or prohibit rentals — check the bylaws.
    • This is not financial, tax, or legal advice. Talk to a lender, an insurance agent, a tax professional and an attorney before you buy.
